Keywords: <span style="color:#ffffffd1; font-family:monospace; font-size:0.75rem;"><i>compositionality, tensor product spaces, universal algebra, quantum formalisms, group theory, theoretical models of memory</i></span>.

My research seeks to uncover the **mathematical principles underlying intelligent systems** -- how continuous neural dynamics can give rise to the discrete, compositional structure of cognition. These efforts are currently organised around four interrelated themes:

1. Compositionality through Abstract Algebra
Compositionality -- the principle that complex meaning arises from the structured combination of simpler parts -- has traditionally been treated as a symbolic property defined over discrete syntactic rules. My work approaches compositionality instead as an algebraic invariant, one that should hold across representational systems, independent of their implementation. Using the tools of universal algebra, I formalise compositional systems as homomorphisms between a syntactic term algebra and a semantic algebra, allowing compositionality to be characterised abstractly and compared across symbolic, neural, and physical systems. This approach clarifies the strengths and limits of structure-preserving representations and motivates more general frameworks that capture context-dependent or non-homomorphic forms of composition, where meaning depends on relational or dynamical context.

2. Tensor Product Spaces as a Unifying Substrate
Tensor product spaces provide a unifying substrate for studying both compositional and non-compositional phenomena. They offer the expressive capacity to represent structured combinations and distributed, interdependent relationships (as in entanglement). I am interested in how the algebraic and geometric properties of these spaces -- such as canonical isomorphisms with dual spaces, rank-1 manifolds, and operator-induced subspaces -- can formalise holism, where a part’s interpretation depends on the configuration of the whole, and emergence, where new properties arise at the composite level. This work seeks to identify the mathematical conditions under which distributed systems can exhibit structured, context-sensitive behaviour without relying on explicit symbolic control.

3. Quantum Mechanics as a Computational Abstraction
The mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics provides a compelling abstraction for reasoning about distributed, contextual, and compositional systems. Concepts such as tensor products, density matrices, and operator algebras separate the space of representational states from the space of transformations that act on them -- much like the separation between mental content and cognitive operations. I am interested in how these tools might serve as computational analogies for cognition, offering a principled way to model context-dependent meaning, uncertainty, and superposition-like representations that are not reducible to classical probability. For instance, the partial trace as a mathematical marginalisation has direct parallels to lossy compression and the forgetting of irrelevant features. Ultimately, I see quantum formalism not as a claim about neural hardware, but as an elegant mathematical language for describing the interplay between structure and dynamics in distributed systems.

4. Group and Representation Theory for Subsystem Discovery
In both physics and cognition, the boundaries of a “part” or “subsystem” are not fixed but defined by the invariances of the system. Group theory and representation theory provide a rigorous way to study these invariances -- how symmetries constrain the geometry of representational spaces and how invariant subspaces correspond to meaningful decompositions of structure. Although this is an area I am only beginning to explore, I am especially interested in how these tools might formalise the process of subsystem discovery: how a distributed system identifies meaningful objects, modules, or relations through its own internal symmetries and transformations.

5. Theoretical Models of Memory and Attractor Dynamics
Beyond representation itself lies the question of how structure is stored and recalled. I am particularly interested in how structured attractors could support the retrieval and cleanup of composite representations through intrinsic dynamics rather than explicit lookup. This leads to questions about representational trade-offs: Should a system store only primitives, risking degradation through recursive unbinding, or all possible composites, at combinatorial cost? Or could the geometry of the energy landscape itself encode the topology of compositional trees, allowing retrieval to unfold along its natural dynamics? More broadly, I aim to connect algebraic structure and dynamical computation, developing principles for how compositional representations can be stably maintained and flexibly retrieved in neural systems.
