Keywords: <span style="color:#ffffffd1; font-family:monospace; font-size:0.75rem;"><i>compositionality, tensor product spaces, universal algebra, quantum formalisms, group theory, theoretical models of memory</i></span>.

My research seeks to uncover the **mathematical principles underlying intelligent systems** -- how continuous neural dynamics can give rise to the discrete, compositional structure of cognition. These efforts are currently organised around four interrelated themes:

1. Compositionality and Algebraic Structure
My research began with an interest in how <b>vector spaces<b> can <b>explicitly encode compositional structure<b> -- how structured, symbolic-like meaning can arise directly within a continuous representational substrate. This led me to frameworks such as <b>Tensor Product Representations (TPRs)<b> and <b>Vector Symbolic Architectures (VSAs)<b>, which formalise compositionality as structure-preserving mappings <b>(homomorphisms)<b> between a syntactic term algebra and a semantic vector space. Over time, my focus has shifted toward a more abstract formulation of compositionality, using the tools of universal algebra to characterise it independently of any particular representational scheme or binding operator. This algebraic perspective treats compositionality as a general invariant property of representational systems, clarifying what must be preserved when structure is learned, represented, or transformed across different levels of abstraction. This approach illuminates the strengths and limits of such systems and motivates more general frameworks that capture context-dependent or non-homomorphic forms of composition, where meaning depends on relational or dynamical context.

2. Tensor Product Spaces as a Unifying Substrate
Tensor product spaces provide a unifying substrate for studying both compositional and non-compositional phenomena. They offer the expressive capacity to represent structured combinations (as in rank 1 tensors) and distributed, interdependent relationships (as in entanglement). I am interested in how the algebraic and geometric properties of these spaces -- such as canonical isomorphisms with dual spaces, rank-1 manifolds, and operator-induced subspaces -- can formalise holism, where a part’s interpretation depends on the configuration of the whole, and emergence, where new properties arise at the composite level. This work seeks to identify the mathematical conditions under which distributed systems can exhibit structured, context-sensitive behaviour without relying on explicit symbolic control.

3. Quantum Mechanics as a Computational Abstraction
The formalism of quantum mechanics -- tensor products for composite state spaces, density matrices, partial traces, and operator algebras -- provides a powerful mathematical language for representing systems that are <b>distributed, contextual, and compositional<b>. I’m interested in how these tools might serve as a computational abstraction for cognition: a way of modelling how global context shapes local meaning, or how representational “states” and the operations acting on them might be mathematically separated. This perspective is not about the brain as a quantum system per se (sorry Penrose!), but about whether the mathematical structures of quantum theory -- its treatment of uncertainty, contextuality, and superposition -- can offer new ways to formalise aspects of intelligence and learning.

4. Group and Representation Theory for Subsystem Discovery
In both physics and cognition, the boundaries of a “part” or “subsystem” are not fixed but defined by the invariances of the system. <b>Group theory and representation theory<b> provide a rigorous way to study these invariances -- how symmetries constrain the geometry of representational spaces and how invariant subspaces correspond to meaningful decompositions of structure. Although this is an area I am only beginning to explore, I am especially interested in how these tools might formalise the process of subsystem discovery: how a distributed system identifies meaningful objects, modules, or relations through its own internal symmetries and transformations.

5. Theoretical Models of Memory and Attractor Dynamics
I’m broadly interested in <b>theoretical models of memory<b> and <b>attractor dynamics<b> -- how distributed systems store, stabilise, and retrieve information through their own recurrent activity. Within this space, I’m particularly drawn to the question of how <b>compositional structures<b> can be stored and recalled. If a memory system stores only its <b>primitive constituents<b> (the leaves of a tree structure), retrieval requires recursive unbinding of some composite down to the leaves -- causing noise to accumulate with depth. But if it stores all <b>possible composites<b>, the memory footprint grows combinatorially (scaling with the Catalan number of possible structures). This trade-off raises a deeper question: could the <b>dynamics themselves<b> somehow encode the <b>structure of compositional trees<b>, so that retrieval unfolds naturally along the geometry of the energy landscape? In such a system, compositional structure would be embedded not in static representations but in the <b>transitions and attractors<b> of its dynamics. I’m drawn to this problem as a bridge between algebraic structure and dynamical computation, allowing the dynamics of memory to mirror the combinatorial structure of thought.
